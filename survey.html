Survey Results

We surveyed 15 UW–Madison students about residence hall accessibility. Most (14 of 15) had lived in a UW residence hall in the past, and 1 lives in one now. Because this is a small group, these results show real student experiences but do not represent every student on campus.

Key findings

  • Laundry was the lowest-rated area (average 2.33 out of 5), followed by storage (2.73) and kitchens (3.00).
  • 5 of 15 respondents (33.3%) lived in a residence hall with no elevator.
  • Elevators came up in 6 of the 11 written answers about difficulties, including no elevator, unreliable elevators, and trouble finding them.
  • Overall, students rated their residence hall's accessibility 3.13 out of 5.

In students' own words

We asked students what accessibility difficulties they have experienced and what they would improve. These are answers from anonymous respondents. Spelling was lightly corrected, and nothing else was changed.

"I twisted my ankle pretty bad over the winter, and it was very hard to get up to my room on the third floor, since there was no elevator."

"No elevator and living on the third floor was tough. I couldn't imagine having a disability and having to navigate that."

"Unreliable elevators, slippery entranceway in winter, very minimal storage in rooms, for laundry frequently the entire lower row of units would be in use."

"Elevators would break sometimes, entrance doors are not light to push open."

"My dorm did not have any accessible doors or elevator options, so if there was anyone with a disability in my building that would need an accommodation, my building in lakeshore would not have been able to work for that individual."

"Signage for different amenities because it is difficult to navigate."

Another respondent, who has accommodations for noise and light sensitivity, described how constant building noise early in the morning and late at night can cause migraines and make it hard to focus. A different respondent described how heavy double entrance doors are hard to open for someone with muscle fatigue. Barriers are not only about wheelchairs and ramps.

Recommendations

Based on what students told us, we recommend:

  • Reliable elevators in every residence hall. One-third of respondents lived in a hall with no elevator, and broken elevators came up repeatedly.
  • Lighter or automatic entrance doors. Heavy and double doors were a barrier for several respondents.
  • Better laundry access. Laundry was the lowest-rated area. Respondents mentioned machines being full and the cost of using them.
  • More accessible storage. Storage was the second-lowest-rated area, and respondents described very minimal storage in rooms.
  • Clearer signs and wayfinding. Respondents had trouble finding elevators and amenities.
  • Larger rooms. Two respondents named bigger rooms as the one thing they would improve.
  • Think about sensory needs. Noise and lighting can be barriers for students with hidden disabilities.
